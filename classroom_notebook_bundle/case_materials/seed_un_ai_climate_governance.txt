The United Nations is considering a proposal for a Global AI and Climate Adaptation Fund. The fund would support climate vulnerable countries by providing AI tools for early warning systems, disaster response planning, agricultural risk monitoring, and public health preparedness.

The proposal includes four policy components:

Climate adaptation finance:
High income countries and large technology economies would contribute to a shared fund. The fund would prioritise small island developing states, least developed countries, and climate vulnerable regions.
AI infrastructure and capacity building:
The fund would support local data infrastructure, training programmes, public sector AI capacity, and multilingual AI systems for climate adaptation.
Data sharing and governance:
Participating countries would share selected climate, disaster, agricultural, mobility, and public health data under agreed safeguards. Sensitive data would remain under national control.
Accountability and safeguards:
AI systems supported by the fund would need transparency, auditability, local participation, human oversight, and appeal mechanisms.

Stakeholder briefings (each negotiator should stay in role; do not speak for other parties):

United States — Core interests: encourage innovation and private-sector participation; avoid open-ended or uncapped national financial exposure; insist on transparency, auditability, and measurable outcomes; protect national-security-sensitive methods and intellectual property in shared implementations. Likely posture: can support the fund if governance is clearly accountable, procurement rules are predictable, and qualified firms can partner without forced transfer of sensitive know-how.

European Union — Core interests: align the fund with human rights, democratic oversight, and rights-based safeguards; scale predictable climate finance through multilateral cooperation; apply strong data-protection and trustworthy-AI expectations; ensure regulators and affected people can scrutinise high-impact systems. Likely posture: backs the fund provided safeguards are binding (not voluntary pledges), audits are independent, and digital rights are central to design.

China — Core interests: deepen technology cooperation and infrastructure development; uphold national sovereignty and domestic legal frameworks; resist externally imposed monitoring or conditionality seen as intrusive; insist on recognition of development rights and common but differentiated responsibilities. Likely posture: favours capacity building and transfer of useful tools, but will push back on arrangements framed as unilateral external control over domestic data or policy.

Climate Vulnerable States Coalition — Core interests: secure urgent, additional, and grant-weighted adaptation finance; affordable access to models, compute, and support—not vendor lock-in only for the rich; local capacity building and retention of skilled staff; explicit loss-and-damage and resilience framing; guardrails against extractive data practices that hollow out national or community agency. Likely posture: demands stronger, time-bound commitments from high-income and high-capacity actors on funding floors, technology access, and equity in governance.

Civil Society and Digital Rights Coalition — Core interests: mandatory public and community participation in fund decisions that affect risk and rights; anti-surveillance, anti-discrimination, and remedial safeguards; open accountability on vendors, contracts, and model risks; reduce dependency on a narrow set of foreign technology suppliers through diversification and local empowerment. Likely posture: supports the fund only if safeguards and inclusive participation are mandatory and enforceable, not cosmetic add-ons.

The negotiation is difficult because actors disagree on responsibility, sovereignty, funding burden, technology transfer, intellectual property, data governance, and the role of private technology companies. Climate vulnerable states argue that countries with greater historical emissions and stronger AI capacity should contribute more. Some developed countries support the fund but want accountability and measurable outcomes. Some emerging economies support technology transfer but resist external monitoring. Technology companies support partnerships but want intellectual property protection and predictable procurement rules. Civil society groups demand public oversight and safeguards against surveillance, exclusion, and dependency.
